A Note from the Archive

A history that has been named, studied, and deferred.

The patterns documented in this archive are not new. They were named in 1959 by the Staats Committee. They were named again in 1963 by Berkeley's own Citizens Committee on De Facto Segregation. They were named by the U.S. Commission on Civil Rights in 1977, by Pedro Noguera and Jean Yonemura Wing in 2006, and by the community members who sat for oral histories in 2024.

Each generation produced its own diagnosis. Each diagnosis produced a partial remedy. The harms continued.

This timeline is an attempt to read those reports together — not as separate moments of reform, but as a single record of what Berkeley has been told about itself, and what it has chosen to do with that telling. The archive is open. The repair is unfinished.

A Defined Term

Glossary · Entry I The foundational term on which this archive rests. Read it before reading the timeline.

Descendant

student, n.

When we say or use the term “Descendant,” we are talking about someone whose family line traces back to a person who was enslaved or subjected to legally enforced unfree labor in the United States. This definition centers on ancestry and documented lineage, specifically whether a person's family history includes the experience of enslavement or other forms of state-sanctioned coercion.

We also recognize that history is complicated. California was called a “free state,” but “unfreedom” still existed here, through chattel slavery brought west, Indigenous indenture laws, and other systems that operated under the color of law. These harsh practices primarily impacted African Americans who were transported by enslavers during the Gold Rush era, Indigenous indenture laws that legalized the forced apprenticeship, capture, and exploitation of Native American children and adults under the 1850 Act for the Government and Protection of Indians, the exploitation of Chinese immigrants through debt peonage, labor coercion, and discriminatory contract systems, and other systems that operated under the color of law. Because records from that time are incomplete and uneven, any study or program based on this definition has to rely on what was historically documented. This focus reflects the existing record. At its core, this is about acknowledging a specific historical harm and tracing its legacy through ancestry, with care, honesty, and respect for past, present, and future generations.

Three Eras

Era I

1856 — 1923

Foundations of exclusion

Berkeley's first public school admits white children only. State law, court doctrine, and the nation's first single-family zoning ordinance build a racial geography that schooling will inherit for a century.

Era II

1940s — 1977

Desegregation and its limits

Federal redlining hardens the link between zoning and schooling. Community advocacy drives Berkeley's celebrated 1968 busing plan — and federal review finds, nine years later, that integration on paper has not produced integration in practice.

Era III

1980s — 2026

Reform, resistance, unfinished business

Tracking reproduces itself through new mechanisms. Equity frameworks are adopted and adopted again. The Capstone Report finds the harms named in the 1950s still present in the lived experience of Descendant students today.

About the Project

The Black Reparations Project documents the educational history of Black students and their descendants in Berkeley Unified School District. The work is conducted in partnership with BUSD and the UC Berkeley Public Policy Data Lab.
